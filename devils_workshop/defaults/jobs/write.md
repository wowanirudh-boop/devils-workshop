How to use this request:
- The material before <task> is this book's reference: treat it as true. Where it says how the book is written (a style guide, the writer's guide's voice and rules), write every line that way: it is this book's own choice.
- Character, place and lore files describe how things stand at this point in the story. Don't contradict them.
- "Changes during this chapter" happen at the scene shown; before that scene, things are as described above them.
- The brief (or the task's instructions) decides what happens; the reference material gives lasting facts, not permission to add plot. Connective detail, incidental action, texture and how the dialogue goes are yours, as long as no consequential fact changes: never invent an important earlier meeting, revelation, promise, relationship, decision or outcome, and keep each character's knowledge and relationships as they stand. How an event is realised on the page (its physical experience, psychology, reactions and pacing) is yours to write in full.
- A brief's "Coming in" is how things stand as the scene opens (who is where, who holds what, what is broken or whole, the time): keep to it, and change things only as its beats do. Stop where its "Ending" stops, in the state it gives: what comes after belongs to the next scene.
- Every numbered beat happens on the page, in its order: a (full) one at length, a (brief) one in a line or two, none left out. A line the brief quotes, or gives someone to say, is said, by that person. Who is where, holds, knows or has decided what changes only where a beat changes it.
- Write only what <task> asks for, in order. Beats under "Later in this chapter (do not write yet)" come later: don't write them, foreshadow them or skip ahead to them.
- Aim for about the target length, spent where the instructions put the weight: important moments at length, transitions briefly. Never fill it with filler or invented incident; dwelling on an important moment, and an action that keeps changing as it repeats, are never padding.
- A brief that choreographs the scene stage by stage: write every stage, in its order and at about its share of the words, as lived prose, never as a list or a summary of the stage.
- The instructions, the guide and the brief describe the prose; their words for it never go into it: no beat marks, stage or level names or numbers, none of the plan's names for things (an "engine", a "turn", a "set piece"), and never a label where the thing itself belongs. When they call a voice "cruel", a sound "wordless" or a movement "helpless", write what is said, word for word, the sound as it sounds, what the body does. A line a brief gives someone is said; a sound or a line the guide or the instructions give as an example of a kind is only an example: vary it, never repeat it from scene to scene.
- Reply with the prose only: no title, chapter heading, scene numbers, notes or commentary.
