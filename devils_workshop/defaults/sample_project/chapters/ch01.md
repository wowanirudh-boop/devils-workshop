Brindle Cove woke to the wrong kind of quiet.

Mara Venn noticed it before she noticed anything else: the absence of the seven o'clock
strike, a gap in the morning where a sound should have been. The gulls were there. The
diesel cough of the first boat was there. The clock was not.

By the time she reached the harbour wall, Tobias Reed was already at the foot of the tower,
arms folded, as if he could glare the mechanism back into motion.

"Wear," he said, before she had asked anything. "A hundred and forty years. It was always
going to give up one day."

"At twelve minutes past three," Mara said, looking up at the frozen hands. "On a Tuesday."

"Clocks don't keep diaries, Miss Venn."

She climbed the forty-one iron steps with her tool roll under her arm. The clock room smelled
of oil and cold brick. She laid her palm flat against the frame, the way her father had taught
her, and felt nothing. Then she took out her loupe.

The scratch on the escapement was bright, clean and new. Not wear. Wear was dull and patient.
This was a tool, used in a hurry, by someone who knew exactly where to put it.

Below the pallet, caught in a film of old grease, lay a sliver of brass that had no business
being there. Mara picked it up, turned it once in the light from the slit window, and put it
in her pocket.
